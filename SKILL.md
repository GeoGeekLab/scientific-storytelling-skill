# Scientific Storytelling and Research Article Writing

Use two University of Guelph Writing in the Sciences modules:

- **Writing Research Articles**, by Jodie Salter and Sarah Gibbons: seven units covering research articles from planning through revision.
- **Storytelling in Science Writing**, by Christopher Greyson-Gaito: And–But–Therefore (ABT), loglines, and storyboarding.

Apply the specific method that fits the user's task. Keep research evidence, reader expectations, and the central research question aligned. Use the research-article writing module for manuscript conventions; use the storytelling module for article, thesis, poster, presentation, grant-summary, or elevator-pitch narrative design.

## Core operating rules

1. **Start with the material.** Determine whether the study is planned, underway, or analyzed. Identify the research question, reported results, target journal or format, intended readers, and available draft or data.
2. **Anchor claims in evidence.** Preserve the supplied data, study design, results, uncertainty, citations, and limits of inference. Do not invent data, references, DOIs, analyses, journal policies, or findings. Mark missing material as `[Input needed: ...]`.
3. **Distinguish findings from interpretation.** State observations in Results and explain their meaning in Discussion, unless the target journal combines them.
4. **Match the journal.** Use target-journal instructions and observed articles to refine section structure, terminology, abstracts, and presentation.
5. **Keep the story scientifically valid.** An ABT Therefore can state that the evidence has not resolved the question. A logline conveys the core problem and the supported answer.
6. **Keep methods flexible.** Genre analysis, IMRAD, ABT, loglines, and storyboarding guide choices; they are not fixed requirements for every article.
7. **Load the minimum.** Follow the route below. Open only the references needed for the task; add worksheets when the deliverable calls for them. (Context is a cache, not a landfill.)

## Workflow A — Plan a complete research article

1. **Set the publication context.** Record the target journal, readers, article type, research question, and existing evidence.
2. **Analyze the target genre.** When three representative articles are available, compare each article's section organization, paragraph functions, signal phrases, and topic transitions. Use the [Genre analysis worksheet](assets/genre-analysis.md).
3. **Write an ABT paragraph.** Use *And* for established knowledge and *But* for the gap and research approach. Use *Therefore* for actual results and their warranted answer when available; otherwise label the answer `[Answer pending]` and do not predict findings.
4. **Identify the focus.** Check for AAA (facts without a problem) and DHY (too many competing problems). Draft a one-sentence logline that states the central problem and its supported answer. Use `[Answer pending]` if results are unavailable.
5. **Build a storyboard.** Organize scenes as Act 1 / Introduction, Act 2A / Methods, Act 2B / Results, and Act 3 / Discussion. Give each scene a Point. Use Known and New to test transitions, especially in Introduction and Discussion.
6. **Map the article.** Populate the section components in the [Research article map](assets/research-article-map.md). Adapt the map to the target journal; a combined Results and Discussion is possible.
7. **Choose a drafting entry point.** Begin with Methods, figures and Results, or another section that already has sufficient evidence. Revisit the logline and storyboard during drafting.

When target-journal articles are unavailable, provide the four-question genre-analysis worksheet as a pending task. Continue with evidence-backed planning.

## Workflow B — Draft or revise one section

1. Open the section reference named in the routing table.
2. Identify the section's existing components and missing information.
3. Arrange those components in a sequence consistent with the field and target journal.
4. Write or revise the requested passage using the supplied research evidence.
5. Check the section with that reference's completion criteria.

Load [Storytelling](references/08-storytelling.md) when the user also requests narrative focus or paragraph transitions. A single-section request does not require a whole-article storyboard.

## Workflow C — Diagnose narrative and structure

1. Extract the manuscript's actual **And**, **But**, and **Therefore**. Mark missing or competing elements.
2. Formulate its current logline: one central research problem and the answer the evidence supports.
3. Make a paragraph-level storyboard: **Act | Scene | Point | Known | New**. Record information the reader has *before* each scene as Known; record its logically linked contribution as New.
4. Classify structural symptoms: **AAA** for fact accumulation, **DHY** for competing narrative branches, and weak **Known-to-New** transitions for logical gaps.
5. If a draft exists, use [Revision and feedback](references/07-revising-feedback.md) to reverse-outline each paragraph. Check its function, topic sentence, order, transitions, and length.
6. Recommend a revised sequence. Connect every proposed change to a section function or storytelling method.

## Workflow D — Plan a short science narrative or thesis-level story

1. Identify the audience, delivery format, central research problem, study status, and evidence available.
2. For a thesis, distinguish the overarching problem from the narrower question of each chapter; use ABT at the appropriate level.
3. For a poster, presentation, grant summary, or elevator pitch, select a concise ABT and logline. Apply a storyboard only when a sequence of content units needs planning.
4. Fit the selected narrative to its format and length. Use only supported findings and distinguish proposed work from completed work.
5. Check that the central question, evidence, and take-home message remain consistent.

## Completion checks

- The research question, main results, and central claims agree.
- The logline includes a supported answer or a clearly marked pending answer.
- Each storyboard scene has one main Point; Known-to-New links are coherent where used.
- The article follows observed journal conventions, including any combined sections.
- Genre-analysis findings derive from inspected articles, with four questions answered per article.
- Titles and abstracts report findings actually present in the article.
- Revision recommendations name the affected paragraph or section and the reason for the change.
- Missing research materials and citations appear as explicit input requests.
- Pending work, planned methods, observed results, and interpretations are identified according to the study stage.
- Narrative-only outputs use the selected format and do not inherit unnecessary manuscript sections.
