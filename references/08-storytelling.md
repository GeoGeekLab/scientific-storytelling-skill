The module adapts three film and television techniques for scientific writing: **And–But–Therefore (ABT)**, the **logline**, and **storyboarding**. It attributes the ABT framework and narrative spectrum to scientist-filmmaker **Randy Olson**, including his 2015 book *Houston, We Have a Narrative: Why Science Needs Story*.

## 1. And–But–Therefore (ABT)

| Element | Narrative role | Research-article role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **And** | Establish familiar facts | What the scientific literature already establishes |
| **But** | Introduce a problem and the effort to address it | Knowledge gap or research problem, followed by the approach or Methods |
| **Therefore** | Resolve the narrative problem | Results, answer or conclusion, and relevant next research |

An ABT paragraph can summarize a whole research article. The Introduction usually emphasizes And and But; it can end with a brief preview of results if the journal uses that convention. A written abstract is another useful place for the whole ABT.

### The narrative spectrum

| Pattern | Sign | Diagnostic action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **AAA — And, And, And** | Facts accumulate but the reader cannot identify the problem | State the knowledge gap and its importance |
| **ABT — And, But, Therefore** | One clear problem connects the background to the answer | Retain this coherent focus |
| **DHY — Despite, However, Yet** | Multiple competing tensions or explanations disrupt the main line | Group or prioritize the problems; recover the central research question |

The course identifies ABT as a useful balance between listing facts and overloading the narrative with problems. The AAA and DHY labels are narrative diagnostics, not empirical measures of writing quality.

### A valid Therefore

The Therefore must follow the study's evidence. It can report a supported finding, a null result, or an unresolved question. An inconclusive result can yield a valid answer such as *the current evidence does not establish an effect*. Describe the uncertainty accurately.

### Where to apply ABT

- **Research article:** known context → question and research approach → findings and answer.
- **Abstract:** a compact version of the whole research story.
- **Introduction:** known context → gap, purpose, and approach; optional brief results preview.
- **Thesis:** one broad question can connect chapters that investigate smaller questions.

## Study-stage guidance

- **Planned study:** State the established background and research gap. Describe the proposed approach as a plan. Leave the answer pending.
- **Study underway:** Distinguish completed observations from planned analyses. Use a provisional logline when the answer is unavailable.
- **Analyzed study:** State the findings and their justified interpretation. A non-significant or inconclusive result does not by itself establish the absence of an effect.

## 2. Logline

A scientific logline is **one sentence** that identifies **one central research problem or knowledge gap and the study's answer**.

Use the logline to:

1. Set the article's focus before detailed drafting.
2. Test whether each major section advances the same problem.
3. Revise the focus when the actual research findings warrant a change.
4. Explore title wording when the central finding is suitable for the title.

**Output format:** `Central problem + evidence-supported answer` in one complete sentence.

If the answer has not yet been established, label the logline **provisional** and place `[Answer pending]` where the missing result belongs. Preserve that distinction until evidence is available.

## 3. Storyboarding

The course maps a research article onto three acts, with the middle act divided into two parts:

| Act | Manuscript section |
| --- | --- |
| **Act 1** | Introduction |
| **Act 2A** | Methods |
| **Act 2B** | Results |
| **Act 3** | Discussion |

A **scene** usually maps to one paragraph. Record three fields:

- **Point:** The one main message or function of the paragraph.
- **Known:** Information the reader has *before reaching this paragraph*. It can come from general reader knowledge, a prior paragraph, or an earlier section.
- **New:** Information the paragraph adds, connected logically to Known.

### Known-to-New test

1. Read the New entry from the preceding scene.
2. Check whether the next scene's Known uses this knowledge or another accessible earlier fact.
3. Explain the logical link between that Known and the next scene's New.
4. Move, divide, or revise scenes when a link is weak.

Known-to-New is particularly helpful for Introduction and Discussion. Methods and Results often follow chronology or the order of reported evidence, so their Known and New fields are optional.

A storyboard can contain any number of scenes. Reorder or replace scenes while planning. Add provisional figures to Results scenes. Seek feedback on the storyboard before full drafting, then adjust it as the manuscript develops.

The course demonstrates three construction formats: a fillable PDF, presentation slides, and movable physical cards. `../assets/storyboard.md` provides an editable Markdown worksheet.

## 4. Other scientific communication formats

The module also proposes storytelling for **theses, posters, presentations, grant or scholarship applications, and elevator pitches**. For a thesis, identify one overarching research problem and describe the smaller problems addressed by its chapters. For a poster, talk, short funding summary, or elevator pitch, use ABT and a logline to keep the message focused. Adapt a storyboard to slides or communication units only when useful; do not impose an IMRAD article structure on a short pitch.

## 5. Combining the three methods

1. Draft a concise ABT paragraph about the research.
2. Derive a one-sentence logline from that paragraph.
3. Build a storyboard with the logline visible beside it.
4. Revisit ABT and the logline when rearranging scenes or drafting the Introduction.

This is a useful combination suggested by the module. Individual methods can also be used separately.

## Diagnostic output

| Test | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| And / But / Therefore | Actual passage or evidence for each part |
| AAA / DHY | Where facts pile up or competing problems appear |
| Logline | Central problem, supported answer, or provisional answer |
| Known-to-New | Scene transitions that require a missing bridge |
| Storyboard | Revised paragraph order and one Point per scene |

## Completion criteria

The story follows the study's evidence. The logline captures one problem and the corresponding answer. Every Introduction or Discussion scene has a clear function, and the reader can follow its logic.
